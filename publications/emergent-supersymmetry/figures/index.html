How to read the flow

One dot can hide two fixed points. Change the angle to see them separate, then follow the paper’s arrows to find which one attracts the flow at long distances.

Read the plot in three moves

Each position represents a choice of interaction strengths. The arrows show how the strengths change at longer distances, a process called renormalization-group flow. A fixed point is a set of strengths that stays put.

Three dots stand for six fixed points

Panel A shows the Yukawa coupling h and one scalar coupling a. Each marked dot is a pair with the same values on those axes. The two members differ in the third coupling, b.

A second view separates the pair

Panel B holds the Yukawa coupling at its interacting fixed-point value, h = 4/√5, and puts b on the horizontal axis. The upper pair splits into the supersymmetric point [susy] at b = −0.20 and the saddle [ns₂] at b = −0.30. The lower pair splits too. The two zero-Yukawa fixed points lie outside this slice.

Nearby paths approach [susy]

Play the paths in panel B to see different starting couplings approach the same point. At the saddle, the flow approaches along one direction and moves away along another. The mass is tuned to criticality in both plots.

Exact coordinates and flow conventions

Use h = λy/(4π√ε), a = λ1/[(4π)2ε] and b = λ2/[(4π)2ε]. The rescaled infrared time is τ = ε ln(μ0/μ) for d = 4 − ε, with ε > 0. The arrows point toward smaller μ, hence longer distances.

At one loop, the equations for h and a depend only on those two couplings. At fixed h = 4/√5 and a = 1/5, the remaining equation is db/dτ = −2(b + 1/5)(b + 3/10). This gives attraction along b at [susy] and repulsion along b at [ns₂]. At the lower pair, [ns−] is a saddle and [ns+] repels in both directions within the quartic plane.

What the flow tells us

When does the symmetry emerge?

In the SU(N) adjoint and SO(N) S2 models, the stable interacting point shown by the large-N flow is supersymmetric. After tuning to criticality, small changes in the interaction strengths can wash out as you look farther away.

The SO(N) S2–A2 model is nonsupersymmetric. In the planar limit, its shared observables agree perturbatively with those of the supersymmetric models after matching couplings. The scalar and fermion scaling dimensions give one way to see the agreement take shape.
